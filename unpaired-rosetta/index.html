Overview

Aligning Two Embedding Spaces Without Pairs

We show that models trained independently in different modalities (e.g., image and text models) are similar enough that they can be coarsely aligned without pairs using a single, orthogonal map. This is challenging because the models have no shared coordinates or known correspondences and potentially have different numbers of samples and dimensions. Our method uses that coarse distances are often similar in both modalities.

The plot shows 1,000 images and 1,000 captions from two separate halves of MS COCO. At the start, the images (circles) and the captions (triangles) lie in their own spaces. The animation then applies the learned map, and the colours show that each image ends up near captions about the same kind of content.

Main findings

  • Vision and language models can be aligned without pairs, even across different datasets.
  • When few pairs (≤1000) are available, our method outperforms existing few-pair methods by a large margin.
  • predicts how well the unpaired alignment works, also for scientific and medical modalities.
  • Our generic method is competitive with or better than other domain-specific methods on their respective unpaired benchmarks.

Our Method

Wasserstein Procrustes + Geometric Initialization

We take images from one half of the dataset and captions from the other half, so no image is paired with its original caption. We embed them with independently trained vision and language models. Our method then learns one global orthogonal map, a single matrix that moves every image embedding into the caption space.

Circles are image embeddings from DINOv2 B/14, and triangles are caption embeddings from Qwen3-8B. The two sets come from separate, non-overlapping subsets of MS COCO, and they start out in different spaces with different numbers of dimensions. There is no known correspondence between the dimensions of the two spaces or between the samples in the two sets. Hover over or tap any point to see its image or caption.

Geometric initialization

We cluster the images and the captions separately. We then match the image clusters to the caption clusters so that the similarities between cluster centers agree as well as possible, which is a small quadratic assignment problem. Repeating this matching on random batches and averaging the results gives a coarse correspondence between the two sets.

The plot shows 8 clusters. The method uses 30 clusters and 30 repetitions.

We turn the coarse correspondence into a one-to-one matching between individual images and captions. The matching is one optimization step on the objective. then finds the one orthogonal matrix that best fits all matched pairs at once.

Image and text embeddings from independently trained models often share enough structure to be aligned coarsely without any image-caption pairs. The alignment works for many model combinations, and it works even when the images and captions come from different datasets. Its quality varies with the model combination and the caption style. For example, Qwen3 gen stays close to chance on the long, detailed captions of SPC, DCI, and DOCCI, and we analyze this difference further in the paper.

Generating Images From Captions

The Map Made Visible

We use image generation to make the alignment visible. We embed a caption with the MPNet language model, and our map moves this embedding into the image space of DINOv2 B/14. A small , which was trained only on ImageNet images and never on captions, then generates an image from the mapped embedding. The caption can therefore only influence the image through our map. The goal is to show what the map preserves, and not to compete with text-to-image models that are trained on millions of image-caption pairs.

Without any pairs, the generated images already show a reasonable scene, but they often miss fine details. Adding a few known pairs sharpens individual objects and scene attributes.

FOSCTTM

FOSCTTM stands for the fraction of samples closer than the true match. Take an image and its true caption. Count how many of the other captions are closer to the image than the true one, and divide by the number of captions. Averaged over all images, this gives one number between 0 and 1.

A value of 0 means the true caption is always the nearest. A value of 0.5 is what random guessing gives. We report it on the 40,504 pairs of the COCO validation set, which the method never sees during training.

CKA

Centered kernel alignment compares two embeddings of the same samples by the pairwise similarities within each space. It does not need the spaces to share dimensions or axes, and it does not change under orthogonal transformations or a uniform scaling of either space. We compute it on paired data only as an analysis tool. The method itself never uses pairs.

A value of 1 means both spaces arrange the samples in the same way, up to an orthogonal transformation and a scale. A value of 0 means the two arrangements are unrelated.

Gromov-Wasserstein

Gromov-Wasserstein matching compares two sets of points that lie in different spaces. It looks for a matching between the two sets under which the distances between points in one set agree as well as possible with the distances between the matched points in the other set.

Because it only compares distances within each set, it needs no shared coordinates. Our read-out takes one step of this optimization, which turns the coarse correspondence of the initialization into a one-to-one matching.

Orthogonal Procrustes

Orthogonal Procrustes finds the orthogonal map that moves one set of points closest to a matched second set, measured by the sum of squared distances. An orthogonal map keeps all distances and angles between points, so it can only rotate and reflect the space.

The best map has a closed-form solution from a singular value decomposition, so it is fast to compute.

Hungarian matching

The Hungarian algorithm finds the one-to-one matching between two sets with the highest total score, given a score for every pair. We use the similarity between the mapped images and the captions as the score, and we run the algorithm on random batches of samples.

RAE diffusion model

A representation autoencoder (RAE) uses the frozen DINOv2 B/14 image encoder as its encoder, and a decoder turns the encoder's features back into an image. A diffusion model generates images in this feature space.

We condition the diffusion model on the mean DINOv2 embedding of an image, and we train it on ImageNet images only, so it never sees a caption. A caption can therefore only change the generated image through our map.

R²

R², the coefficient of determination, is the share of the variation in FOSCTTM that a straight line in CKA explains. A value of 1 means that all points lie on the line, and a value of 0 means that the line explains none of the variation.

Spearman correlation

The Spearman correlation ρ compares the ranks of two quantities instead of their values. It is 1 when a higher CKA always comes with a higher FOSCTTM, and it is −1 when a higher CKA always comes with a lower FOSCTTM.

A value close to −1 means that sorting the combinations by CKA almost sorts them by how well they align.
